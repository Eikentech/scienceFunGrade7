LEVEL 01 · MEASURE · 15–20 MINUTES

Give your observations a number.

Two cups can feel equally warm and still contain different amounts of thermal energy. Good science starts by separating the ideas we measure.

Heat ≠ temperature

Temperature describes how hot or cold something is. It is related to the average energy of particles’ random motion. Heat is energy transferred because of a temperature difference.

The SI unit of heat is the joule (J). The SI unit of temperature is the kelvin (K); Celsius (°C) is common in daily life. We write 300 K, without a degree symbol.

Heating has several effects

It can raise temperature, cause expansion, or change a substance’s state. During melting or boiling at a fixed pressure, energy can change the state without raising the temperature.

A calorie is another energy unit: 1 cal ≈ 4.2 J for school calculations. Thus 10 cal ≈ 42 J. Use the approximation specified in a question.

Predict: An ice–water mixture stays near 0°C while its ice melts. Is energy still entering? Yes: the incoming energy changes ice into liquid. A temperature reading alone does not tell the whole story.

LAB 1 · CALIBRATE A THERMOMETER

A scale needs reliable reference points.

For a Celsius scale, melting pure ice gives the lower reference point, 0°C. Steam above boiling pure water gives the upper point, 100°C, at standard atmospheric pressure. Divide that interval into 100 equal degrees.

In a liquid-in-glass thermometer, the liquid expands relative to the glass and rises in the narrow tube. The bulb exchanges energy with the object until they reach thermal equilibrium.

There are 20 equal intervals between 0°C and 100°C. What is the reading now?

Show the method

Each interval = (100 − 0) ÷ 20 = 5°C. Multiply the number of intervals above zero by 5. Read a real scale with your eye level with the top of its liquid column to avoid parallax.

F = (9 × C ÷ 5) + 32 · C = (F − 32) × 5 ÷ 9 · K = C + 273.15. Some school problems round 273.15 to 273; follow the question. *At standard atmospheric pressure.

Temperature rise: A rise of 10°C is a rise of 10 K, but 18°F. Add 32 only when converting a temperature reading, not a temperature difference.

Choose the right thermometer

Clinical thermometer

Measures body temperature over a narrow range. A traditional glass clinical thermometer commonly spans about 35–42°C. Its constriction helps hold the reading after removal. Digital models sense temperature electronically.

Laboratory thermometer

Measures a wider range, often −10 to 110°C in school labs, though instruments vary. Read a traditional laboratory thermometer while its bulb remains immersed; keep the bulb off the container’s bottom and sides.

Use digital or non-mercury instruments with adult supervision. Never use a clinical thermometer for boiling water or heat a sealed container.

LEVEL 02 · EXPAND · 15–20 MINUTES

A tiny change can move a switch.

A bridge needs room to move. A jar lid can loosen when warmed. A thermostat can switch off a heater. All use thermal expansion.

Three dimensions to watch

Linear expansion: an increase in length, as in a rail.
Area expansion: an increase in surface area, as in a sheet.
Volume expansion: an increase in volume, as in a liquid.

These describe what we measure; a solid usually expands in all directions. For most materials, heating increases average particle separation. The particles themselves do not get larger.

Make a fair comparison

Expansion depends on the material, starting size, and temperature change. Compare identical starting sizes under the same temperature rise.

Gases generally show much larger volume changes than solids for a similar rise when pressure is kept constant. If a gas is trapped in a rigid sealed container, heating instead raises its pressure.

The ring puzzle: Heat a metal ring uniformly while it is free to expand. Its hole gets bigger too! Picture every length in the ring scaling up together.

LAB 3 · BIMETALLIC THERMOSTAT

Two joined metals. Different expansion.

Metal A expands more than metal B for the same temperature rise. They are bonded together, so the strip curves. On heating, A becomes the longer outside curve; the strip bends toward B.

Our model opens the heater’s switch at 60°C and closes it again below 60°C. Real thermostats can use different arrangements and separate on/off thresholds.

LAB 4 · WATER BREAKS THE USUAL RULE

What happens between 0°C and 4°C?

Liquid water has its greatest density at about 4°C. As it warms from 0°C to 4°C, it contracts. Above 4°C, it expands as it warms. This unusual behaviour is called anomalous expansion.

same mass of water

Illustration only: volume changes are greatly exaggerated and not to scale. Treat the 0°C sample as liquid water at its melting point.

Why can a pond freeze on top?

As surface water cools to 4°C it becomes denser and can sink. Water cooler than 4°C is less dense and tends to stay near the top. Ice is less dense still, so it floats and forms an insulating surface layer. Water below can remain liquid.

Expansion in the real world

Leave room to move

Bridge joints allow expansion and contraction. Gaps in some rail systems prevent compressive stresses from becoming excessive. Modern welded rails manage those stresses in other ways.

Ball and ring

A cool metal ball fits through a ring. Heating only the ball can make it too large to pass. Cooling the ball restores the fit. Heating the ring instead enlarges its hole.

LEVEL 03 · ENGINEER · 15–20 MINUTES

Use the route. Control the result.

Heat can move down a metal rod, circulate with a fluid, or travel as radiation through empty space. Engineering means knowing which route matters.

Conduction

Energy transfers through matter without bulk movement of that matter. Metals conduct well. In a solid, neighbouring particles transfer energy; mobile electrons also help conduction in metals.

Use: a cooking-pan base.

Convection

Energy moves with flowing liquid or gas. Natural convection often occurs because warmer fluid is less dense and rises while cooler fluid sinks. Fans and pumps can force fluid movement too.

Use: circulating hot water.

Radiation

Energy travels as electromagnetic waves. No material medium is needed. Objects emit thermal radiation; hotter objects generally emit more.

Use: solar heating.

Three pathways, one kitchen: A hot hob conducts energy into a pan. Water circulates by convection. Hot surfaces also emit radiation. A real object can gain or lose energy by several routes at once.

Absorb, reflect, emit

Dull, dark surfaces generally absorb and emit thermal radiation more effectively than bright, shiny metal surfaces at the same temperature. That helps explain a solar cooker’s black interior and a flask’s shiny surfaces. Surface finish matters most for radiation; it does not stop conduction or convection.

Plan a fair radiation experiment

Use two otherwise identical containers, one dull black and one shiny, with equal amounts of water at the same starting temperature. Place them at equal distances from the same radiant source. Measure temperatures after equal times. Change only the surface finish; keep material, size, water mass, and position comparable. Ask an adult to handle any hot apparatus.

Choose a thermometer, thermostat, sea breeze, or flask. Explain what changes, where energy goes, and why the design works. Draw arrows and label the important parts.

Revision card: the six ideas to remember
  1. Heat is transferred energy; temperature is a measure of hotness.
  2. F = 9C/5 + 32; K = C + 273.15. Temperature differences do not use the +32 offset.
  3. Most materials expand when heated; free, uniformly heated holes grow too.
  4. A heated bimetallic strip bends toward the metal that expands less.
  5. Liquid water is densest at about 4°C.
  6. Conduction needs matter; convection needs a flowing fluid; radiation can cross a vacuum.
